An interactive exploration of how Victorian infrastructure and daily habits intersect across our rail networks and active cycling corridors.

✅ Safe / Physically Separated

  • Protected bike lane (on-road): Physically separated from vehicular traffic by bollards, curbs, or structural barriers.
  • Shared use path (off-road): Off-road path completely removed from motorized vehicle corridors, shared with pedestrians.
  • Separated path (off-road): Premium off-road infrastructure dedicated exclusively to bicycles, isolated from both cars and pedestrians.

⚠️ Unsafe / Mixed Traffic

  • Basic painted lane: Segregated by paint alone. Provides zero physical intrusion protection.
  • Buffered painted lane: Features an extra painted dead-zone strip, but still completely relies on driver compliance.
  • Shared bike/parking lane: Permits active car parking, introducing extreme risks of blind-side "dooring" accidents.
  • Intermittent/informal: Non-exclusive roadside pictograms shunted against the curb with broken lines.
  • Shared street (sharrows): Arrow symbols painted directly in center lanes, explicitly forcing riders to negotiate mixed heavy traffic.

Section B: The Pulse of the Week: Workdays vs Weekends

While the rail network is defined by sharp, symmetrical commuter peaks, bicycle traffic pulses with a more consistent rhythm throughout the day. This difference in utility extends to the weekend, where cycling proves significantly more resilient by retaining nearly 70% of its weekday volume, compared to just 52% for the rail network.

Section C: Under the Hood of Cycling Infrastructure

Paint on a road isn't a bike lane. Does better infrastructure actually attract more riders? Correlating physical path classifications against counter sites shows a clear trend: safe, separated off-road trails attract higher, steadier rider volumes. Meanwhile, on-road lanes see unpredictable speeds and lower usage, highlighting the safety concerns of mixing bicycles with vehicle traffic.

Section D: Geographic Flows and Seasonal Shifts

Where do we come from, and how does the weather change our habits? Grouping station patronage geographically tracks the massive morning migration from the suburbs into the CBD. Tracking this across a full calendar matrix reveals how Melbourne's cold, wet winter months cause a sharp drop in active cycling, while train passenger volumes remain steady year-round.
